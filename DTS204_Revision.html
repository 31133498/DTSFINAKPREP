1(b)Handling missing values (3 marks)

This checks whether F2 is at least 50; if true it returns Pass, otherwise Fail.

2(a)Mean, Median, Mode (3 marks)

Define and differentiate between Mean, Median, and Mode.

Mean is the arithmetic average — sum of all values divided by their count; sensitive to extreme values (outliers). Median is the middle value when data are ordered; not affected by extreme values, so it is preferred for skewed data. Mode is the most frequently occurring value; the only measure of central tendency usable for purely categorical data.
Exam tipThe 'why' matters: Mean is outlier-sensitive, Median isn't, Mode works on categories. That's the differentiator they're testing.
2(b)Standard deviation (3 marks)

Explain how standard deviation measures variability, and interpret a high vs low value.

Standard deviation measures how far, on average, individual values lie from the mean — the square root of the variance. A low standard deviation means values cluster tightly around the mean (consistent, predictable data); a high standard deviation means values are spread widely (more variable, less predictable data). Example: a low SD in Salary means most staff earn close to the average; a high SD means salaries vary widely.
Exam tipAlways give a real-world interpretation example — definition alone rarely gets full marks.

2(e)Independent samples t-test (3.5 marks)

Explain the concept of an independent samples t-test, including its assumptions.

An independent-samples t-test compares the means of a continuous variable (e.g. Salary) between two separate, unrelated groups (e.g. Male vs Female) to test whether the difference between their means is statistically significant. Assumptions: (1) the dependent variable is continuous; (2) observations are independent; (3) the dependent variable is approximately normally distributed in each group; (4) the two groups have approximately equal variances (homogeneity of variance, often checked with Levene's test).
Exam tip4 assumptions, always in this order: continuous DV → independence → normality → equal variances (Levene's).

(2-tailed): if it is less than 0.05, the difference in means between the two groups is statistically significant at the 5% level.
Exam tip'Sig. (2-tailed)' IS the p-value — state this explicitly, examiners look for that exact phrase.
2(g)t-test vs ANOVA (2 marks)

Explain the difference between t-test and ANOVA, and when each should be used.

A t-test compares the means of exactly two groups. ANOVA compares the means of three or more groups in a single test, avoiding the inflated Type I error rate that would come from running many pairwise t-tests. Use a t-test for two groups (e.g. Male vs Female); use ANOVA when comparing three or more (e.g. three Departments).
Exam tipThe WHY matters: running many t-tests inflates Type I error — that's the real reason ANOVA exists, not just convenience.
3(a)Multiple regression model (3.5 marks)

Write the general form of a multiple linear regression model and define each term.

y = β0 + β1x1 + β2x2 + … + βnxn + ε

where y is the dependent variable (Productivity); x₁,…,xₙ are the independent variables/predictors (Training Hours, Experience, Job Satisfaction); β₀ is the intercept (predicted y when all x = 0); β₁,…,βₙ are the coefficients, each representing the change in y for a one-unit change in that predictor, holding the others constant; ε is the random error term.
Exam tipWrite the formula AND define every single symbol — each symbol left undefined can cost a mark.

3(d)R-squared and Adjusted R-squared (2 marks)

Interpret the meaning of R-squared and Adjusted R-squared.

R² is the proportion of variance in the dependent variable explained by the model (0 to 1); higher generally means a better fit. Adjusted R² corrects R² for the number of predictors, only increasing when a new predictor improves the model more than expected by chance — the reliable metric for comparing models with different numbers of predictors.
Exam tipKey phrase: Adjusted R² 'penalizes' adding useless predictors — plain R² never decreases even with junk variables.
3(e)Multicollinearity (2 marks)

Explain the concept of multicollinearity and how it can be detected in SPSS.

Multicollinearity occurs when two or more independent variables are highly correlated with each other, making it difficult to isolate each predictor's individual effect and destabilising the coefficient estimates. In SPSS it is detected via Collinearity diagnostics in the Linear Regression Statistics dialog, which reports Tolerance (low values, e.g. < 0.1, indicate a problem) and VIF (Variance Inflation Factor; values above 10 indicate serious multicollinearity).
Exam tipMemorize the exact numbers: Tolerance < 0.1 = problem. VIF > 10 = serious problem.
3(f)Assumptions of linear regression (3 marks)

State and explain at least three assumptions of linear regression.

1. Linearity — the relationship between predictors and the target is genuinely linear. 2. Independence of errors — residuals are independent of one another, no repeating pattern. 3. Homoscedasticity — residuals have constant variance across all predictor levels. 4. Normality of residuals — the errors are approximately normally distributed.
Exam tipMnemonic: LINE — Linearity, Independence, Normality, Equal variance.
3(g)Interpreting a p-value (2 marks)

A regression output shows a predictor with p-value = 0.03. Explain what this implies at a 5% significance level.

Since 0.03 < 0.05, the predictor is statistically significant at the 5% level — we reject the null hypothesis that its coefficient is zero. This means the predictor has a genuine (non-zero) relationship with the dependent variable, and this effect is unlikely to be due to chance.
Exam tipAlways say the comparison explicitly (0.03 < 0.05) before stating the conclusion — showing the comparison is where marks live.
4(a)List vs Tuple (3 marks)

Differentiate between these two examples of a sequence data type: List and Tuple.

A list (e.g. [1,2,3]) is written with square brackets and is mutable — it can be changed after creation (append, remove, sort). A tuple (e.g. (4,5)) is written with parentheses and is immutable — it cannot be changed after creation. Tuples are slightly faster and can be used as dictionary keys; lists are used for data that will change.
Exam tipBrackets: [ ] = list = mutable. ( ) = tuple = immutable. That's the whole exam trick.

4(c)Surface area of a cylinder (4 marks)

Write Python code to find the total surface area of a cylinder closed at both ends having diameter of 28 cm and height 7 cm. (π = 3.142)

Total surface area of a closed cylinder = 2πr(r + h), with r = diameter/2 = 14.
pi = 3.142
d = 28
r = d / 2  # 14
h = 7
tsa = 2 * pi * r * (r + h)
print(tsa)  # 1847.496
Exam tipAlways convert diameter to radius FIRST (r = d/2) before plugging into the formula — this is the #1 mistake in cylinder questions.

Visualization

Every matplotlib chart follows this EXACT skeleton, memorize the shape not the specifics:
  1. import matplotlib.pyplot as plt (always first, always exactly this)
  2. Prepare your data as lists/variables
  3. Call the plotting function: plt.bar(), plt.scatter(), plt.plot(), or plt.pie()
  4. plt.title("...") — always a quoted string
  5. plt.xlabel("...") and plt.ylabel("...") — always quoted strings (pie charts don't need these)
  6. plt.legend() — only needed if you used label= anywhere
  7. plt.show() — always last, always exactly this
The debugging questions in this exam ONLY ever break these 4 things:
  • Missing import matplotlib.pyplot as plt entirely
  • Unquoted strings where text is expected (labels, titles) — e.g. plt.title(Sales) instead of plt.title("Sales")
  • Mismatched brackets — (A, B, C] instead of ['A','B','C']
  • Wrong function name — pl.show() instead of plt.show(), or a stray - instead of = like label-Data
Scan every debug question for exactly these 4 patterns first, before reading the code line by line.

Section A — Data Foundations (Types of Data)

A1What is the Big Split in types of data? Name the two main categories.

Discrete data is countable, whole values (e.g. number of students). Continuous data can take any value within a range (e.g. height, weight).
A3Define nominal data and give an example.
Nominal data has categories with no inherent order, e.g. sex (male/female) or nationality.
A4Define ordinal data and give an example.
Ordinal data has categories with a meaningful order but unequal gaps between them, e.g. class of degree (First, Second, Third) or a Likert scale.
A5Define interval data and give an example.
Interval data is ordered with equal gaps between points but has no true zero, e.g. temperature in Celsius (0°C is not "no temperature").
A6Define ratio data and give an example.
Ratio data is like interval data but has a true zero meaning "none", e.g. weight, length, or temperature in Kelvin.

It is exactly what the Measure column in SPSS's Variable View asks for (Scale, Ordinal or Nominal), and it decides which statistical test is valid for that variable.

Section O — Regression

O1Define regression and explain what 'supervised' and 'continuous' mean in this context.
Regression is a supervised learning technique used to predict a continuous outcome. Supervised means the model learns from labelled examples (inputs paired with known outputs); continuous means the predicted quantity is a number on a continuous scale, not a category.
O2What is the cost function (MSE), and what does Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) do?
MSE is the average of the squared differences between actual and predicted values; it measures how wrong a candidate line is. OLS is the method that finds the coefficients minimising this MSE, i.e. the line of best fit.
O3State and explain the four assumptions of linear regression (LINE).
Linearity – the relationship is genuinely linear. Independence of errors – residuals show no repeating pattern. Normality of residuals – errors are approximately normally distributed. Equal variance (Homoscedasticity) – errors have constant variance across predictor levels.
O4Interpret β0 and β1 in the model y = β0 + β1x.
β₀ (the intercept) is the predicted value of y when x = 0. β₁ (the slope/coefficient) is the change in y for a one-unit increase in x.
O5What does R² measure, and why is Adjusted R² preferred for multiple regression?
R² measures the proportion of variance in the target explained by the model (0 to 1). Adjusted R² is preferred for multiple regression because ordinary R² never decreases when a feature is added, even a useless one, whereas Adjusted R² penalises irrelevant features.
O6Explain overfitting versus underfitting, and how regularization helps.
Overfitting is when a model learns the training data too well, including its noise, performing well on training data but poorly on unseen data (high variance). Underfitting is when a model is too simple to capture the pattern, performing poorly on both (high bias). Regularization adds a penalty on large coefficients to the cost function, discouraging overly complex models and reducing overfitting.
O7Differentiate Ridge (L2) regression from Lasso (L1) regression.
Ridge shrinks coefficients toward zero but rarely to exactly zero. Lasso can shrink some coefficients to exactly zero, performing automatic feature selection; it is preferred when many predictors are suspected to be irrelevant.

O9What is multicollinearity and how is it detected in SPSS?
Multicollinearity occurs when two or more independent variables are highly correlated with each other, destabilising coefficient estimates. It is detected in SPSS via Collinearity diagnostics, which report Tolerance (low values, e.g. below 0.1, indicate a problem) and VIF (values above 10 indicate serious multicollinearity).

Section P — ANOVA

P1What is ANOVA, and why not simply run many t-tests instead?
ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) tests whether there are significant differences between the means of three or more groups using a single F-test. Running many separate t-tests instead would inflate the Type I error rate, the chance of finding a difference that isn't really there.
P2Explain between-group variance versus within-group variance and how they form the F-statistic.
Between-group variance measures how much group means differ from one another (and the overall mean). Within-group variance measures how much values vary inside each group. The F-statistic is the ratio of between-group to within-group variance: F = MSbetween / MSwithin.
P3Differentiate one-way from two-way ANOVA, and explain what an interaction effect is.
One-way ANOVA examines one factor's effect on one dependent variable. Two-way ANOVA examines two factors and their interaction on one dependent variable. An interaction effect exists when the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other factor.
P4Name the three ANOVA assumptions and the test used to check each.
Independence of observations (ensured by study design); Normality of residuals, checked with the Shapiro-Wilk test; Homogeneity of variances, checked with Levene's test.
P5What alternative test is used when ANOVA data are non-normal? When variances are unequal?
When data are non-normal, use the Kruskal-Wallis test (non-parametric alternative). When variances are unequal, use Welch's ANOVA.
P6Run a one-way ANOVA with SciPy and interpret the p-value in code. 
from scipy.stats import f_oneway
f_stat, p_value = f_oneway(group_A, group_B, group_C)
print(f_stat, p_value)
if p_value < 0.05:
    print('At least two groups differ significantly')
P7Run a two-way ANOVA with statsmodels including the interaction term. 
import statsmodels.api as sm
from statsmodels.formula.api import ols
model = ols('Growth ~ C(Fertilizer) + C(Sunlight) + C(Fertilizer):C(Sunlight)', data=df).fit()
print(sm.stats.anova_lm(model, typ=2))
P8What does a significant Tukey HSD post-hoc test tell you that the ANOVA itself does not?
A significant ANOVA only shows that at least one group differs; Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test compares all pairs of groups to reveal exactly which specific pairs differ, while controlling the overall error rate.

Final Tips

Read this section last, right before you walk in.
  1. Every 'list the steps' question is graded on SEQUENCE. If you know the destination menu but forget one click in between, you still lose marks — say EVERY click, even ones that feel obvious.
  2. For any 'differentiate X from Y' question, structure your answer as two clearly separated sentences, one per term — don't blend them into one confusing paragraph.
  3. Nested IF: ALWAYS test the highest threshold first. This is the single most common formula mistake under time pressure.
  4. '2 groups = t-test, 3+ groups = ANOVA' — if a question mentions comparing means across categories, count the categories FIRST before choosing your test.
  5. Debug questions only ever break 4 things: missing import, unquoted strings, mismatched brackets, wrong function name (pl vs plt, or a stray - instead of =). Scan for these 4 first.
  6. Always name the exact SPSS output term when asked to interpret — 'Sig. (2-tailed)' IS the p-value, R² is in the 'Model Summary' table, coefficients are in the 'Coefficients' table.
  7. When a question gives marks split (e.g. '2 advantages and 2 limitations'), match that exact split in your answer — don't give 3 and 1.
  8. Cylinder formulas: Volume = πr²h. Total Surface Area (closed) = 2πr(r+h). Don't confuse them — volume has NO (r+h) term.
  9. If a diameter is given instead of radius, ALWAYS divide by 2 first before plugging into any formula — this is a deliberately placed trap in nearly every geometry question.
  10. For Excel multiple regression, remember your X (independent) variables MUST be in ADJACENT columns for the ToolPak to accept them — rearrange columns if needed before running.
  11. Keep an eye on the clock: skim ALL questions first, answer the ones you know cold immediately, come back for anything requiring more thought — never lose easy marks by running out of time on a hard question first.
